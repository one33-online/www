## Emotional Intent (the "three-word" exercise)

Richard's answer to how he wants people to feel engaging with One33:

- **Emotional, aggrieved, and grateful** — aggrieved at the injustice, grateful that a group of people is prepared to do something about it.
- The anchor word is **unlocked**. He doesn't just want a feeling named; he wants the visitor's emotions *unlocked* — which then causes them to think, and pushes them to place that feeling somewhere constructive.
- Possible adjacent words: *curious*, *intriguing*.
- Rationale: *"Unless it hits us deep within our soul we will not be moved to action."* The site should confront the visitor with a real problem, not soothe them.
- A "tell" that it worked: you remember where you were when you engaged with it. Richard can name where he was when he first read the copy and his emotions welled up.

These three feelings become the brand words, replacing the earlier working placeholders (*Resolved. Grounded. Human.*): **Emotional. Aggrieved. Grateful.**

**Takeaway for us:** treat *unlocked → think → act* as the emotional arc, and revisit design/copy decisions against these three brand words — does each decision line up, and is anything pulling away from that direction?

## Imagery Direction

The central question Josh raised: how to use human imagery without landing in the cliché NPO space — art-directed/crafted, real township photography, or a mix?

Richard leans clearly toward **human imagery**. Key points:

- He is moved most when he sees **himself reflected in another human in radically different circumstances**. A structural shot (a shack, a broken building) reads as "someone should fix that"; a *person* reads as "this is someone like me, and this is not right." The human face carries the story.
- Distance matters — seeing another human (rather than a literal mirror of his own life) is what lets him feel it.
- Reference images that move him: a mother walking her child to school through dirt; a malnourished boy, no shirt, *hopeful and skeptical*; the 1976 Soweto uprising photograph — *"the story is written on a human face."*
- The specific image he has in mind: a child sitting in front of their creche / ECD centre — leaning gate, dust, half a peeling sign on the wall — waiting. *"How is anyone supposed to move forward in life when that's your reality?"*

**The principle: a person in a context.** Dignified, real, human-led — not a structural/poverty shot, and not a glossy NPO stock feel.

**No explicit privilege contrast.** Richard does not want the comparison spelled out (e.g. golf course / clean breakfast table beside township scenes): *"I don't need a picture of what I had the privilege of."* The person-in-context should carry the tension on its own.

> Nuances Session 1's "no poverty imagery": hardship *context* is welcome when it's centred on a dignified human subject — what's off the table is the de-humanised structural shot and the explicit have/have-not comparison.
